Chapter One · The Life

A voice raised in devotion

Pervez Fateh Ali Khan was born on 13 October 1948 in Lyallpur — the Punjabi city now called Faisalabad — the fifth child and first son of Ustad Fateh Ali Khan, a revered musicologist, vocalist and qawwal. His family, whose roots lay in Jalandhar, belonged to the historic Qawwal Bachchon ka Gharana and had practised qawwali, the devotional music of Sufism, for some six hundred years.

His father at first hoped the boy would become a doctor, and Nusrat was steered toward his studies. But the music of the house — the harmonium, the tabla, the rising chorus of the family party — was inescapable. He learned tabla, harmonium and the architecture of khayal singing at his father's side, and after Fateh Ali Khan's death in 1964, under his uncles Mubarak Ali Khan and Salamat Ali Khan.

His first public performance came at his father's chelum — the memorial gathering forty days after his death — when he was fifteen. Britannica records his first public performance as a qawwal in 1966, singing with his uncles. In 1971, after Mubarak Ali Khan's death, he became head of the family party, first leading it in public at a Radio Pakistan music festival that March. The ensemble became known as Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, Mujahid Mubarak Ali Khan & Party — and the modern history of qawwali began.

i.

The training

Qawwali is not simply sung; it is built. Nusrat mastered the classical grammar beneath it — raga, sargam solfège, rhythmic cycles counted against the hand-claps of the party — then stretched it: longer improvisations, quickening tempos, and vocal runs of a speed and range that left fellow musicians astonished. Britannica notes he increased the tempo of the form to connect with contemporary audiences, without surrendering its devotional core.

ii.

The breakthrough

Signed in the early 1980s by Oriental Star Agencies of Birmingham, he became a phenomenon among South Asian audiences in Britain. Then, in 1985, he performed at Peter Gabriel's WOMAD festival on Mersea Island, Essex — his first performance before a predominantly Western audience. Listeners who understood no Urdu stayed for hours. The voice needed no translation.

iii.

The world stage

Gabriel signed him to Real World Records. He sang on Passion (1989), the soundtrack to Martin Scorsese's The Last Temptation of Christ; toured the United States from 1989; scored the Indian film Bandit Queen (1994); and sang with Eddie Vedder for Dead Man Walking (1995). Relentless touring built a global audience — and exhausted the body that carried the voice.

iv.

The final journey

In August 1997, travelling from Lahore to Los Angeles for a kidney transplant, he was taken ill in London with kidney and liver failure. He died of a sudden cardiac arrest at Cromwell Hospital on 16 August 1997, aged 48, at the height of his fame. His body was flown home to Faisalabad, where thousands gathered to mourn.

v.

The succession

Having no son, he had trained his nephew — Rahat Fateh Ali Khan, son of his brother Farrukh — who had sung beside him in the party since childhood. Rahat inherited the leadership of the ensemble and carried the family's qawwali into a new century, introducing his uncle's voice to yet another generation.

vi.

The afterlife of the voice

Time magazine placed him in its 2006 list of Asian Heroes, nine years after his death. In 2024, Real World Records released Chain of Light — four traditional qawwalis recorded in April 1990, lost in the label's archive for decades and rediscovered in 2021 — and the world heard Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, at his peak, sing again.

Chapter Seven · Collaborations

The meetings that changed two worlds

Western musicians did not “discover” Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan — he arrived complete, carrying six centuries of music. What the collaborations gave both sides was a new language: qawwali's ecstatic architecture meeting the recording studio, the film score, and rock.

The Patron · Real World Records

Peter Gabriel

Gabriel heard him at WOMAD in 1985 and brought him to Real World Records. In 1989 Nusrat's voice soared over Passion, Gabriel's soundtrack for Martin Scorsese's The Last Temptation of Christ — for many Western listeners, their first encounter with qawwali. Real World went on to release his great traditional albums and, in 2024, the rediscovered Chain of Light.

Passion (1989)Shahen-ShahChain of Light (2024)

The Producer · Infinite Guitar

Michael Brook

The Canadian guitarist and producer built two landmark fusion records around the voice: Mustt Mustt (1990), whose title track in Massive Attack's remix became a club classic, and Night Song (1996). Brook also produced the April 1990 traditional sessions that became Chain of Light, and returned to mix them for release in 2024.

Mustt Mustt (1990)Night Song (1996)Chain of Light (2024)

The Rock Voice · Pearl Jam

Eddie Vedder

For Tim Robbins' film Dead Man Walking (1995), Nusrat recorded with Pearl Jam's Eddie Vedder — most memorably The Long Road, where the two voices circle one another across languages. Vedder remained a lifelong admirer, and the recording introduced Nusrat to a generation of rock audiences.

The Long Road (1995)Dead Man Walking OST

The Cinema · India & Hollywood

Film music

He composed the music for Shekhar Kapur's Bandit Queen (1994), and his recordings were woven into the soundtracks of Natural Born Killers (1994) and Dead Man Walking (1995). Playback singers across India and Pakistan studied his phrasing; his own voice, producers learned, could not be imitated — only invited.

Bandit Queen (1994)Natural Born Killers (1994)

The Remix Culture

Massive Attack & the dancefloor

Massive Attack's remix of Mustt Mustt carried the voice into British club culture, while producers and DJs across South Asia built an entire remix scene around his recordings — an early proof that devotional music, treated with respect, could move a dancefloor without losing its soul.

Mustt Mustt — Massive Attack Remix

The Heir · The Family Party

Rahat Fateh Ali Khan

His nephew Rahat, trained at his side in the party from childhood, assumed its leadership after 1997. Through Rahat's own international career — and through the 2016 Coke Studio performance of Afreen Afreen by Rahat and Momina Mustehsan — the family's music reached its largest audience yet, and new listeners travelled back to the source.

Chapter Nine · Legacy

The king of kings of qawwali

The title was given by audiences, not publicists: Shahenshah-e-Qawwali — the King of Kings of Qawwali. Jeff Buckley called him “my Elvis” and performed his music live; singers from Lahore to London still measure themselves against recordings he made in a single take. He took a sacred, regional art and — without diluting it — made it a world language.

His influence runs through contemporary qawwali, Pakistani and Indian film music, British electronic music, and the global “world music” movement his Real World recordings helped to define. Every year, on the anniversary of his death, Faisalabad gathers at his grave; every day, somewhere, a voice attempts the climb he made sound effortless.
